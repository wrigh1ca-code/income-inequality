Briefing · US inequality

Americans are better off at every rung — and the rungs are farther apart

Is inequality rising? It depends on the yardstick. Below, the same households are measured four ways, each with the strongest bull case and bear case the data support.

Measure 1

Market income: everyone gained, the top gained twice as fast

What it captures: what the economy pays households before government — wages, business and investment income. What it misses: taxes, transfers, health benefits, household size and the ability to borrow or save.

Bull case

  • Real income rose at every percentile Census tracks: the 10th by 59%, the median by 53%.Census P60-286, Table A-4a
  • Median household income hit a record $87,460 in 2025, up 2.6% in a year.Census, Income in the United States: 2025
  • Bottom-fifth average pre-transfer income grew from $19,000 to $28,500 (1979–2023, real).CBO, Distribution of Household Income, 2023

Bear case

  • Growth rises with each rung: +45% at the 40th percentile vs +130% at the 95th.Census P60-286, Table A-4a
  • The top 1% share of pre-tax income rose from 9% to 16% (1979–2023); the bottom fifth's fell from 5% to 4%.CBO, 2023
  • Only about 50% of children born in the 1980s out-earn their parents, vs ~90% of those born in 1940.Chetty et al., Science 2017
Measure 2

After taxes and transfers: the gap halves, but the very top still pulls away

What it captures: resources households actually command — market income plus Social Security, Medicaid, SNAP and refundable credits, minus federal taxes. What it misses: saving and borrowing, the value of owned homes and cars, state and local taxes.

Bull case

  • Taxes and transfers shrink the top-to-bottom fifth ratio from about 14× to 6×.CBO, 2023
  • The bottom fifth's share of after-tax income held at 8% from 1979 to 2023.CBO, 2023
  • Counting all resources, absolute poverty fell from 19.5% to 1.6% (1963–2019); the official rate only to 10.5%.Burkhauser, Corinth, Elwell & Larrimore
  • The uninsured rate was 8.0% in 2024, near a record low, vs ~15% before the ACA.Census CPS ASEC; Peterson Foundation

Bear case

  • The top 1% share of after-tax income still rose from 7% to 13%.CBO, 2023
  • The middle three fifths lost 5 points of after-tax share since 1979.CBO, 2023
  • Much of the bottom's gain comes from transfers, not earnings — and is exposed to policy changes such as the 2025 reconciliation act.CBO
Measure 3

Consumption: living standards are far more equal than paychecks

What it captures: what households actually spend and use, smoothing temporary dips; the measure most economists prefer for well-being. What it misses: survey under-reporting (worst at the top), quality improvements, and leisure and health.

Bull case

  • The top fifth earns about 16× the bottom fifth but spends only about 4×.BLS CE 2025
  • Since the 1960s the 90/10 ratio rose 26% for after-tax income but just 7% for consumption.Meyer & Sullivan
  • Consumption poverty fell from 33.8% to 6.0% (1980–2022).Meyer & Sullivan, 2022 report
  • Low-income computer ownership rose from 16% to 56%; phones from 13% to 80%; TV prices fell 98% since 1997.Census SIPP, ACS; Pew; BLS CPI via Our World in Data

Bear case

  • Aguiar & Bils find consumption inequality closely tracked income inequality, 1980–2007, once survey error is corrected.American Economic Review, 2015
  • The costly services rose fastest: college tuition more than tripled since 1997; childcare, medical care and housing outpaced inflation.BLS CPI via Our World in Data
  • Nearly 30% of households under $25k had no vehicle in 2022, vs under 4% above $100k.FHWA, 2022 NHTS
  • Life expectancy rose 2.3 years for top-5% men (2001–14) but 0.3 for bottom-5% men.Chetty et al., JAMA 2016
Measure 4

Wealth: the widest gap, widening — even as the bottom half got richer

What it captures: security and opportunity — home equity, retirement accounts, stocks and businesses, minus debt. What it misses: Social Security and pensions' promised value, human capital; very sensitive to asset prices.

Bull case

  • Bottom-half net worth per household rose about 50% after inflation, 1989–2025 ($40k → $60k).Fed DFA; author's calculation
  • Every income group's typical family is wealthier than in 1989; the middle fifth's median is up 81%.Fed SCF
  • Housing is the bottom half's main asset (~50% of assets), and the 2019–22 home-price boom produced the largest median wealth gain on record.FRED Blog; Fed SCF 2022

Bear case

  • The top 1% hold a record 32.5% of wealth; the bottom half 2.3%, down from 3.5% in 1989.Fed DFA via 24/7 Wall St.
  • Bottom-half wealth per household was flat for 17 years, then fell to about $7k in 2011.Fed DFA; author's calculation
  • Top-10%-income families' median wealth rose 278% vs 28% for the 20th–40th percentile.Fed SCF
  • The top 1% own 50.9% of stocks held outside retirement plans; the bottom half own 5.2% of 401(k)-type assets.Fed DFA
Verdict

Both stories are true; the measure you pick decides which one you tell

Absolute living standards rose for the bottom half on almost every measure: real income at each percentile, after-tax resources, consumption, durable goods, health coverage and, since 2019, wealth.

Relative position worsened on most measures: the top captured an outsized share of market income and especially of wealth, and mobility and longevity gains lag at the bottom.

The gap between measures is itself the finding. Taxes and transfers halve income inequality; consumption data shrink it further; wealth data magnify it.

Still contested: how much consumption inequality really rose, how to price services versus goods, and whether today's wealth gains at the bottom outlast the housing cycle.
